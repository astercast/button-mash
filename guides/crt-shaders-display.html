Make pixels look right

CRT shaders & display science: why a 1994 game looks harsh on your 4K TV, how artists secretly painted for the blur, and the settings that bring the glow back — plus the input-lag truth nobody's marketing mentions.

01The problem: drawn for a screen you don't own

Every pixel artist of the 8-bit and 16-bit era painted on a CRT — a screen that blurred. Composite video smeared colors sideways, the electron beam bled light into neighboring phosphors, and scanlines darkened every other row. Artists knew this and exploited it: dithering (checkerboard patterns of two colors) was drawn specifically to melt into a third color on a CRT. Sonic's waterfalls, the transparency effects in SNES games, the "more colors than the hardware had" tricks — all of them assumed the blur.

A modern LCD shows you the raw pixels instead: razor-sharp, unblended, with every dither checkerboard exposed as the cheat it was. That's why "accurate" emulation can look wrong — you're seeing the artwork without the display it was designed for. Shaders put the display back.

02Anatomy of the CRT look

The "CRT look" is five effects stacked, and good shaders model all of them:

Scanlines
The beam drew the picture line by line; the gaps between lines read as dark horizontal stripes. The single most recognizable CRT trait — and the one cheap "CRT filters" usually stop at.
Aperture grille vs. shadow mask
How the tube separated colors. Sony's Trinitron used vertical grille stripes (with two faint horizontal damper wires visible on real sets); most other tubes used a shadow mask of tiny dot triads. Shaders offer both — it's the difference between "arcade monitor" and "living-room TV."
Phosphor glow & bloom
Bright pixels bled light into their neighbors. This soft bloom is what melts dithering into new colors — the effect that makes the whole exercise worthwhile.
Curvature & vignette
Tube faces bulged; corners dimmed. Subtle in good shaders, gimmicky when overdone.
NTSC artifacting
Composite video's color encoding created rainbow fringing and dot crawl. Some shaders model it; most people leave it off. Taste.

03Shaders: the look, recreated

In RetroArch, shaders live under Quick Menu → Shaders → Load Shader Preset. The shader pack ships three families: slang (Vulkan/Direct3D, the modern path), glsl (OpenGL), and cg (legacy — ignore it). Pick the family matching your video driver, then try presets in this order:

  • zfast-crt — lightweight, runs on anything, captures 80% of the look. The honest default.
  • crt-easymode — a step up in grille/scanline modeling, still cheap.
  • crt-guest-advanced — the enthusiast favorite: deeply tunable (mask type, bloom, brightness), moderate GPU cost.
  • crt-royale — the reference simulation, famously heavy. Gorgeous on a real GPU, a slideshow on integrated graphics.

Shader cost is real: the heavy presets are doing per-pixel physics. If frames drop, step down the preset ladder before blaming the emulator. And remember — this is taste, not correctness. Some players prefer razor pixels. The only wrong answer is not knowing the option exists.

04The scaling problem (integer vs. blur)

A SNES drew 256×224 pixels. Your screen is 3840×2160. Something has to stretch the picture, and the two honest options are:

Integer scaling
Scale by whole numbers only (2×, 3×, 4×…). Every original pixel becomes an identical block of screen pixels — perfectly even, zero shimmer. The cost: the picture won't exactly fill your screen (black borders top/bottom). Purists accept the borders.
Bilinear / smooth scaling
Stretch to fill, blending fractional pixels. Fills the screen, but uneven pixels shimmer when the picture scrolls — the "wobbly" look that bothers some people enormously and others not at all.

Our Player offers an integer-scale toggle in its display options: it snaps the engine canvas to whole-pixel multiples of the game's native resolution with crisp pixelated rendering. Pair it with the CRT scanline overlay for the classic combo — sharp pixels, soft light.

05Input lag: the honest numbers

At 60 frames per second, one frame is 16.7 ms. A real console on a CRT responded in roughly 1–2 frames (~17–33 ms) from button press to photon. A modern chain adds up:

  • Emulator: ~1 frame of inherent latency (the game reads input, draws the frame).
  • Display processing: 1–3+ frames on a typical TV unless Game Mode is on. This is the big one — a TV's motion smoothing and upscaling can add 50–100 ms by itself.
  • Wireless controllers: a few ms for Bluetooth, more for cheap 2.4 GHz dongles. Wired is still king.
  • V-sync: up to 1 frame to avoid tearing.

Total on a careless setup: 5–8 frames, ~100 ms — the difference between "tight" and "swimming." The fixes, in order of impact: Game Mode on (or a monitor instead of a TV), wired controller, then emulator-side tricks. If a game feels off and you can't name why, measure the chain before blaming the emulator.

06Run-ahead & black frame insertion

Two advanced tools, both with honest trade-offs:

Run-ahead (RetroArch)
The emulator simulates 1–2 frames ahead of what it shows, then rolls back when real input arrives — effectively deleting a frame or two of emulator latency. It works, and on 8/16-bit cores the CPU cost is trivial. The catch: it needs a deterministic core (same requirement as netplay), and it can't fix your TV's processing lag — only the emulator's slice.
Black frame insertion (BFI)
CRTs drew each frame as a flash of light; LCDs hold each frame until the next (sample-and-hold), which your eyes read as motion blur. BFI strobes black frames between real ones, restoring CRT-like motion clarity. The costs: it halves brightness and needs a 120 Hz+ display to avoid flicker. Lovely for scrolling shooters; overkill for RPGs.

07Refresh rates: 59.73 vs. 60

Here's a detail that drives perfectionists mad: the GBA doesn't run at 60 fps. It runs at 59.73 fps; the SNES at ~60.09 Hz; PAL consoles at 50 Hz. Your display runs at exactly 60 (or 120/144). The mismatch means the emulator must occasionally duplicate or drop a frame — a tiny periodic stutter, most visible in smooth side-scrollers.

Three answers, in ascending order of fussiness: ignore it (most people never notice), enable variable refresh rate (G-Sync/FreeSync) so the display follows the emulator instead of the reverse, or use a display mode matching the console's rate. Emulators handle this far better than they used to — but now you know what that one stutter per second is.

08The practical recipe

For most people, most of the time:

  1. Game Mode on (TV) or a monitor. The single biggest latency win.
  2. Integer scaling if you like crisp pixels (our Player's display options), or a zfast-crt/crt-guest preset if you want the glow.
  3. Wired controller for anything timing-sensitive.
  4. Run-ahead: 1 frame in RetroArch for 8/16-bit cores — free latency reduction.
  5. Stop there. The remaining gains are real but small, and the games deserve your attention more than the settings menu does.
