Retro games for every player

An accessibility guide: the games of the '80s and '90s shipped with zero accessibility features — but the emulator layer around them is full of tools the originals never had. Here's the honest map.

01The honest baseline

Let's not romanticize it: a 1991 platformer has no remappable controls, no colorblind mode, no subtitles, no difficulty slider, and text the size of a postage stamp. Accessibility as a discipline barely existed when these games were made. That's the baseline — and it's also the good news, because everything in this guide is added by the emulator around the game, which means it works on the entire library at once, no per-game support needed.

This guide makes no medical claims and offers no diagnoses — it's a toolbox. Take what helps, ignore the rest.

02Hands: remapping, turbo, one-handed play

  • Remap everything. Retro controllers had fixed layouts; emulators don't. Our Player's Controls tool remaps Player 1's keyboard and gamepad bindings per system — put jump where your hand wants it, not where 1991 put it. RetroArch does the same per core.
  • Turbo / autofire. Games that demand 10 presses a second (shoot-'em-ups, I'm looking at you) become playable with a turbo toggle on the fire button. It's a standard emulator feature, not a cheat — it replaces a physical capability with a setting.
  • One-handed layouts. With full remapping, many games can be condensed onto one hand: d-pad + two buttons on adjacent keys, or a gamepad's face buttons clustered under one thumb. Turn-based RPGs and puzzle games are the friendliest starting points.
  • Controller choice matters. A modern gamepad with good ergonomics beats a keyboard for long sessions; a keyboard beats a gamepad for precise menu work. Use both — emulators let you mix inputs freely.

03Eyes: color, contrast, and size

  • Colorblindness: the GBA's washed-out palette is the classic case — the color correction option in GBA cores restores the contrast the artists intended for the dim original screen, which often fixes "I can't tell these apart" moments. For red/green confusion in specific games, some cores and shaders offer palette adjustments; when they don't, your OS-level color filters apply to the emulator window too.
  • Size: integer scaling (see the CRT & display guide) makes pixels crisp at large sizes; your OS magnifier or browser zoom handles the rest. Fullscreen is always one key away.
  • Contrast: CRT shaders with strong scanlines can reduce readability — if text is hard to read, drop the shader before anything else. Sharp pixels beat pretty pixels when your eyes are working hard.
  • Photosensitivity: retro games flash. A lot. Boss transitions, damage flashes, and title screens from the era predate modern flashing guidelines. Emulators can't fix this per-game — if flashing is a problem, preview games with videos first, keep sessions short, and sit farther from the screen.

04Ears: what sound actually carries

Here's a quiet advantage of retro games: they carry very little critical information in audio. No voice acting, no audio-only objectives, no directional sound cues that matter. Music and bleeps are atmosphere; the game state is always on screen. For players who are deaf or hard of hearing, the 8-bit and 16-bit library is one of the most naturally accessible catalogs in gaming.

The exceptions are rhythm games (audio is the game) and a handful of games with sound-only secrets. For everything else, playing muted loses almost nothing — and some players prefer it.

05Mind: difficulty is a dial now

Old games were hard — often artificially, to stretch a two-hour game into a rental week. The emulator gives you a difficulty dial the originals never had:

  • Save states turn a 20-minute gauntlet into 20 one-minute attempts. (Our Player's named save slots make this painless.)
  • Rewind (RetroArch) lets you scrub back a few seconds after a mistake instead of replaying the level. The single kindest feature in emulation.
  • Slow motion gives reaction-heavy sections a gentler pace while you learn the pattern.
  • Cheat codes (invincibility, infinite lives) as accessibility tools: they convert a reflex test into a sightseeing tour. The Player's Cheats tool stores them per game.
  • Guides and walkthroughs for the cryptic parts — old games loved hiding progress behind unmarked walls. Looking it up is not failure; it's the intended 1990s strategy-guide economy, now free.

06On "cheating" (it's not)

A word for the purist voice in your head: using assists doesn't diminish the game. The developers of 1991 built for an arcade economy and a rental market; they are not grading you. Save states, rewind, and turbo are how a 2026 player meets a 1991 game halfway — and finishing a game with assists beats bouncing off it with pride. Play the game, not the guilt.

The house rule: the only wrong way to play is the way that makes you stop playing. Everything else is configuration.

07This site's own commitments

We hold the site to the same standard:

  • Keyboard-operable everything — every tool on this site (brackets, party planner, quiz, player controls) works without a mouse, with visible focus.
  • Reduced motion respected — animations and flicker effects are gated behind prefers-reduced-motion; the kids' corner goes further and disables motion unconditionally.
  • Screen-reader structure — semantic landmarks, skip links, aria-live announcements for dynamic results, honest labels on every control.
  • Contrast and size — high-contrast neon-on-dark palette, resizable text, mobile-first layouts.

Found a barrier we missed? That's a bug — tell us and we'll treat it like one.
