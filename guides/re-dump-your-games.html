Reverse engineering guide · Step one

Dump your own games: discs, cartridges, BIOS and firmware

Every honest path to playing an old game somewhere new starts the same way: the game leaves its cartridge or disc and becomes a file on your computer, copied from media you own, by you. This page walks the pattern system by system, at the level a careful first evening needs.

  • Your media, your hardware, your files
  • ⏱ ~18 min
  • Intermediate
Legal lane: own your copy. Dump your own media, from hardware you own, with your own tools. Never download ROMs, ISOs, BIOS or firmware. Every project named here ships without the game; you feed it yours.

1. The pattern underneath every system

Three things repeat everywhere. First, a way to read the media: a cartridge dumper, a console with homebrew, or an ordinary computer disc drive. Second, a verification habit: good dumps are checked against published checksums (the Redump project for discs, No-Intro for cartridges), so you know your copy is clean rather than hoping. Third, patience with versions: US version 1.0 and EU version 1.1 are different binaries, and decompilation projects in particular want the exact one they name.

2. Cartridges: GB, GBA, N64, SNES and friends

USB cartridge dumpers read a cartridge over a cable and hand you the ROM file; the same hardware often dumps save files, which is half the point. Button Mash's Rip your own cartridges guide is the full walkthrough and this page will not duplicate it. The one addition for reverse engineering: keep the dump's checksum next to the file. Decomp projects ask for it by name.

3. PlayStation and PlayStation 2 discs

PS1 and PS2 discs are CDs and DVDs, and many computer drives read them directly into .iso or .bin/.cue images with ordinary ripping tools. Two habits matter: rip CDs as BIN/CUE if the game uses CD audio (a plain .iso quietly loses the music tracks), and consider converting finished rips to CHD on your computer with MAME's chdman tool, one smaller file, same game. The PS2 BIOS is a separate job: the community pattern is a memory card running the Free McBoot exploit on your own console, which can then write the BIOS to USB. Your console, your BIOS; there is no other honest source.

4. Sega CD and Saturn: the BIOS is the game file's partner

Both systems' emulators want a BIOS dumped from your own hardware before their discs mean anything. The dumping pattern is the same story as the PS2: homebrew or a transfer tool on the real console copies the firmware out. The browser Player's BIOS picker exists for exactly these files, and the rule never changes: dumped from a machine on your shelf, or not at all.

5. Nintendo DS firmware

Most DS games boot in emulators without extra files, but some want the ARM BIOS files and the firmware from a real DS. The established pattern dumps them from your own handheld using a flashcart and a small homebrew dumper. If a game refuses to boot in the Player, this is the fix, and the Player's DS note says so at the moment it matters.

6. GameCube and Wii discs

These discs do not read in ordinary PC drives. The community pattern is a Wii or GameCube running homebrew (CleanRip is the name you will meet) writing the disc to SD or USB as .iso, which Dolphin also accepts in its own compressed .rvz form. GameCube games need no BIOS for Dolphin; Wii system behaviour for some titles comes from a NAND backup of your own Wii.

7. Xbox and Xbox 360

Original Xbox discs need the console itself or a compatible drive, and the result still is not bootable as-is: full disc images are converted to XISO format with the extract-xiso tool. xemu additionally wants two small files from your own Xbox, the MCPX boot ROM (mcpx_1.0.bin) and the BIOS flash ROM. Xbox 360 discs dump through the console or a suitable drive into ISO or installed-content folders for Xenia.

8. PlayStation 3

RPCS3 asks for two things before games: Sony's system software, installed from the PS3UPDAT.PUP update file you obtain for your own console, and your games, dumped from your own Blu-rays with a compatible drive or your own PS3. PSN purchases carry per-game licences tied to your account; the emulator's own documentation is the authority on handling yours. Check the RPCS3 compatibility list per game; Playable and Ingame are very different evenings.

9. Verify, label, back up

Finish the way archivists do. Verify each dump's checksum against the Redump or No-Intro databases where your system has one. Name files with the game, region and version. Keep two copies in two places. A verified, labelled dump is the foundation every other page of this guide stands on, and it is the whole difference between a collection and a pile of files.
