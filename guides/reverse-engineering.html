Reverse engineering guide: the games a browser cannot convert

Some games cannot be turned into browser games today, and Button Mash will not pretend otherwise. This guide is the other half of the converter: for every system the Player has to decline, here is the working path, native emulation, decompilation, recompilation, or a source port, using your own copy throughout.

  • The converter's other half
  • Four paths, plainly told
  • ⏱ ~12 min
  • Beginner
Legal lane: own your copy. Dump your own media, from hardware you own, with your own tools. Never download ROMs, ISOs, BIOS or firmware. Every project named here ships without the game; you feed it yours.

1. Why this guide exists

The Converter and the Player run a wide shelf of systems in your browser. They also meet files they cannot run, and when they do, they name the system and send you here. That is by design. A graceful, specific no, with a working path attached, is worth more than a vague maybe.

Every path below starts from the same place: a game you own, dumped by you. Emulators, decompilers and recompilers are legal tools. The game files, and the BIOS and firmware inside your consoles, are yours to extract and nobody else's to give you.

2. Path A: native emulation

The game runs inside an emulator program on your computer. Not browser play, but it is playing your own copy today, and for the big systems it is excellent: PCSX2 for PlayStation 2, Dolphin for GameCube and Wii, RPCS3 for PlayStation 3, xemu for the original Xbox, Xenia for Xbox 360. Each has a compatibility list; check your game before you invest an evening, because per-game support varies more than the marketing of any one project admits.

3. Path B: matching decompilation

A community rebuilds the game's source code by hand, function by function, until compiling their code produces bytes identical to the original game's. A finished decompilation is source code that can then be ported almost anywhere. Two honest facts: the repos contain no game assets, so building one requires your original disc or cartridge, which the build verifies; and a finished decomp is not a finished port, somebody still has to write the renderer, the inputs and the platform code. The matching decompilation page teaches the working loop.

4. Path C: static recompilation

A tool translates the game's machine code into C automatically, ahead of time, and a small runtime stands in for the console. It is newer and far faster than hand decompilation, and it produced the most impressive ports of the last few years: Zelda 64: Recompiled for Majora's Mask in May 2024, and Unleashed Recompiled, a native PC port of the Xbox 360 Sonic Unleashed, in 2025. A recompiled game is ordinary C, so it can in principle be compiled to WebAssembly; today the ports target native first. The recompilation page has the detail.

5. Path D: source ports and engine recreations

The oldest path. Some engines were open-sourced by their makers, id Software's Doom and Quake are the famous ones, and communities have rebuilt hundreds of others. The engine is new; the levels, art and music still come from your copy. ScummVM is the friendliest example: it takes a folder of your adventure game's files, identifies the game, and just runs it, and it is the model for how a folder-based corner should feel. Many of these ports have web builds, one title at a time, which is where most real PC-games-in-a-browser actually come from.

6. Find your system

PlayStation 2: PCSX2 natively, with a BIOS dumped from your own console. GameCube and Wii: Dolphin natively; Wii games that need real motion are the hard half. PlayStation 3: RPCS3 natively, on a strong desktop; a mid laptop will frustrate. Xbox: xemu natively, with two small files dumped from your own Xbox and your discs converted to XISO. Xbox 360: Xenia natively; a Recompiled release, where one exists, beats it. PC games from Windows XP onward: look for a source port first; your licence often also just runs on a modern PC with community patches.

Nintendo Switch gets a deliberate exception, and it is a policy, not an oversight: this guide carries no Switch emulator walkthrough. The lead Switch emulators were shut down under legal action in 2024, and sending you into what replaced them would put you at risk. What we will happily help with is preservation: backing up your own saves from your own console, and keeping the collection safe. Your Switch games play on your Switch.

7. Start at the beginning: your own files

Whichever path your game takes, step one is the same: get your game off its disc or cartridge and onto your computer, legally. The dumping page walks each system's pattern, and Button Mash's older preservation guides, Rip your own cartridges and Keep your games forever, cover the cartridge side in depth.

A note on freshness: decompilation percentages and emulator statuses drift monthly. Figures in this guide carry their date (October 2026) and the live trackers, decomp.dev for decompilation progress and each emulator's own compatibility list, are the authority. These pages get a quarterly pass; a stale percentage is a bug, not a cosmetic issue.
