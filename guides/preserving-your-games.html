Keep your games forever

The preservation guide: verify your dumps with checksums, name them like an archivist, compress them without losing a byte, and store them so they outlive the hardware, your library, kept properly.

  • Your dumps, your archive
  • ⏱ ~20 min
  • Intermediate
Legal lane: this guide is about archiving games you own, dumps you made from your own cartridges and discs, plus homebrew. Archiving never means distributing: your dumps stay on your devices, period.

01Why bother: the quiet crisis

Physical media dies. Cartridge save batteries last 15-20 years (many are dead already). Discs rot, slowly for pressed discs, faster for burned ones. Optical drives capable of accurate dumping are going extinct. And the games themselves are vanishing from sale: the Video Game History Foundation's survey of the reissue market found roughly 87% of games released in the US before 2010 out of print. For most of gaming history, the secondhand shelf is the store.

Preservation starts at home. A verified dump of your own copy, checksummed and backed up, is a game that survives dead batteries, scratched discs, and dead consoles. Nobody else is going to archive your shelf for you.

02The legal frame (brief, honest)

This isn't legal advice, it's the shape of the landscape. Making a personal backup of software you own is treated very differently from distributing copies. The bright line is the same as everywhere on this site: your dumps, your devices, no sharing.

The broader context: in the 2024 DMCA triennial rulemaking, the US Copyright Office rejected a petition to let libraries and archives share out-of-print games remotely (the game-industry association opposed it), with the next rulemaking cycle in 2027. That's the standing legal weather, preservation advocacy continues, but your personal archiving doesn't depend on it. Dump what you own, keep it to yourself, and you're on solid ground.

03Checksums: the fingerprint

A checksum is a short string computed from a file's bytes, change one byte, the checksum changes completely. It's how you answer "is my dump perfect?" without owning a reference copy. The retro world standardizes on CRC32 (quick, traditional) and SHA-1 (stronger), with MD5 in older databases.

Computing one takes seconds:

  • Windows: 7-Zip's file manager shows CRC32/SHA values (right-click → CRC SHA), or certutil -hashfile game.gba SHA1 in a terminal.
  • macOS / Linux: shasum -a 1 game.gba or sha256sum game.gba.

The workflow: dump your cartridge (see the rip-your-own guide), compute the checksum, and compare it against the database entry for your exact game and region. Match = a perfect read. Mismatch = dump again, clean the contacts, try another read, a bad dump is a corrupted archive.

Store the checksum with the file. A tiny text file next to each ROM, game name, system, checksum, date dumped, which cartridge, turns a folder of files into an archive. Future-you will be grateful.

04No-Intro: the cartridge database

No-Intro catalogs cartridge-based games, every known good dump, with checksums. Its DAT-o-matic database is where you look up your dump: find your game and region, compare checksums, and learn the naming convention:

  • Title (Region), e.g. (USA), (Europe), (Japan), (World).
  • Revisions, (Rev 1), (Rev 2) mark re-releases with fixes.
  • [!] = verified good dump. The mark you want. [b] = known bad dump; [o] = overdump (extra junk bytes appended).
  • (Unl) = unlicensed releases, real history, cataloged alongside the rest.

Naming your files this way isn't pedantry, it's how ROM managers (section 7), scrapers, and your future self identify everything automatically. One evening of renaming beats years of mystery files.

05Redump: the disc database

Redump (redump.org) is the No-Intro equivalent for disc-based systems: PlayStation, Sega CD, Dreamcast, and beyond, and disc dumping is genuinely harder than cartridge dumping. The honest version:

  • You need a compatible drive. Not every optical drive can read discs accurately enough for verification. Redump's wiki maintains the compatibility lists; historically this meant specific Plextor and Lite-On models, and as of 2025-2026 the community has expanded the lists with firmware projects like OmniDrive (March 2026) that enable dumping on more Blu-ray drives, including Xbox and GameCube/Wii discs.
  • The modern tool stack is redumper + MPF. Redumper does the precise reading; MPF (from the SabreTools project) wraps it in a friendlier Windows frontend. DiscImageCreator remains respected for tricky formats like GD-ROM. Expect a learning curve and slow reads, accuracy over speed, always.
  • Redump checksums cover raw sectors. CD dumps are verified at the 2352-byte raw sector level, so a differently-ripped ISO of the "same" disc won't match. Follow the guides exactly; the format details matter.
  • Some discs need exotic hardware. GD-ROMs (Dreamcast) famously need specific drives and even disc-swap tricks. If your system needs unobtanium, an honest "not yet" beats a bad dump.

Redump's front page shows recent verified dumps, the project is active, with tens of thousands of discs cataloged. When your dump's checksums match theirs, your copy is bit-perfect. That's the gold standard.

06Compress without losing a byte

PS1 disc images are ~700 MB each; a modest collection is hundreds of gigabytes. Two honest compression paths:

CHD for discs
The MAME project's CHD format (via its chdman tool) compresses disc images losslessly, typically 30-50% smaller, and modern emulators load CHDs directly. Keep the original checksums recorded, since CHD is a conversion, not the archival master.
7z/zip for cartridges
Cartridge ROMs compress well in 7z. One caution: some tools read CRC32 from the zip's central directory rather than the contained file, always hash the extracted file when verifying, not the archive.

The rule: compress for storage, verify from originals. Checksums describe the uncompressed dump; record them before you compress, and you'll always be able to prove the archive is intact.

07ROM managers & DAT files

Once the collection grows past a dozen games, ROM managers (clrmamepro and RomVault are the long-running names) take over the bookkeeping. Feed them a DAT file: No-Intro and Redump publish these as machine-readable catalogs, and the manager will:

  • Verify every file against known-good checksums and tell you what's bad, missing, or misnamed.
  • Rename files to the canonical convention automatically.
  • Flag overdumps, bad dumps, and duplicates.

Managers also enable the 1G1R ("one game, one ROM") pattern: keep a single preferred region/revision per game instead of six regional variants. Your shelf, your rules: 1G1R is about intentional curation, not deletion.

08The 3-2-1 archive

Archivists swear by 3-2-1: three copies, on two different media, one off-site. For a ROM library it's cheap, even a large collection fits on a single modern drive:

  1. Copy 1: the working library your emulators read.
  2. Copy 2: an external drive, synced after dumping sessions. Include the checksum text files, they're the proof, not the decoration.
  3. Copy 3: off-site, cloud storage or a drive at a relative's house. This is the copy that survives fire, flood, and theft.

Two maintenance habits close the loop: re-verify checksums yearly (bit rot is real; a yearly hash check catches it while you still have good copies), and migrate media every ~5 years (drives die sitting on shelves too). An archive you never check is a hope, not a backup.

The whole guide in one line: dump it, checksum it, name it right, compress a copy, keep three copies, check them yearly. Do that and your games outlive you.
